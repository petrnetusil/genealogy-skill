# How to read parish and civil registers – a practical guide

This guide is country-neutral. Archive portals, closing periods and local peculiarities are in
`reference/countries/<country>.md`; international portals in `portals.md`.

## Kinds of books
| Code | Book | Names you will meet | What it contains | Why it is valuable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **B** | births / baptisms | *Liber natorum / baptizatorum*, *Taufbuch / Geburtsbuch*, *registre des baptêmes / naissances*, *doopboek / geboorteregister*, *kniha narozených* | date of birth and baptism, house/address, child, parents (later also their parents and residence), godparents, midwife | sex, legitimacy, the mother's parents → the next generation |
| **M** | marriages | *Liber copulatorum / matrimoniorum*, *Trauungsbuch*, *registre des mariages*, *trouwboek / huwelijksakten*, *kniha oddaných* | date, groom and bride (age, status, religion, residence), their parents, witnesses | **the best first target** – opens two families at once |
| **D** | deaths / burials | *Liber defunctorum / sepultorum*, *Sterbebuch / Totenbuch*, *registre des sépultures / décès*, *begraafboek / overlijdensakten*, *kniha zemřelých* | date of death and burial, house, name, age, cause, sometimes spouse/parents | age → estimated birth year, status (widow of…) |
| **I** | index | *Index*, *Register*, *table (décennale)*, *klapper / tafel*, *rejstřík* | name → date / page | quick search; **indexes are incomplete** – a name missing from an index proves nothing until you have read it column by column (Reading the script) |
| **C** | census / household lists | *Seelenbuch / Status animarum*, *Volkszählung*, *recensement*, *bevolkingsregister / volkstelling*, *sčítání lidu* | whole households with ages or birth dates, origin, religion, trade | puts a family together in one place and year |

In the data, record kinds are `B`, `M`, `D`, `C` and `O` (other source); index entries are recorded with the kind
of event they point to and a note that it is an index (copy, not the original).

**Church vs. civil registers.** Churches kept registers from the 16th–17th century (Catholic registers became
general after the Council of Trent, 1563; Protestant ones often earlier). The state took over at different times:
France and the territories it ruled (Belgium, Netherlands, Rhineland, Luxembourg) introduced **civil registration**
in the 1790s–1811; Prussia 1874, the German Empire 1876; Hungary (incl. Slovakia) 1895; Austria 1939;
Czechoslovakia 1950. Where civil registers exist, use them first for the 19th–20th century (well indexed, uniform
forms) and church books before them. Details per country in the country guides.

Other sources: censuses, land and manorial records (who owned which house – they link generations through the
property), military records, citizenship/settlement rights, notarial deeds, death notices, gravestones.
See `methodology/beyond_registers.md`.

## What a typical entry looks like
Forms changed over time. Before printed columns, entries are free Latin (or vernacular) sentences; after reforms
(Habsburg lands 1784, France 1792, Netherlands 1811…) they follow printed columns. Read the column headings first.

**Baptism / birth** – columns typically: number · date of birth and baptism · house number / street · name of the
child · religion · sex · legitimate / illegitimate · father (name, trade, residence, from ~1780s often his parents)
· mother (maiden name, her parents) · godparents (name, trade, residence) · midwife · priest; margin: later
legitimation, marriage, death, name change.
*Latin example:* "Die 3 Martii 1760 baptizatus est Joannes, filius legitimus Wenceslai Novak, agricolae ex Lhota
No 5, et Annae uxoris eius. Patrini: Georgius Dvorak et Catharina uxor." → "On 3 March 1760 John was baptised,
legitimate son of Wenceslas Novák, farmer from Lhota no. 5, and his wife Anna. Godparents: George Dvořák and his
wife Catherine."

**Marriage** – date · house · groom (name, trade, age, religion, single/widower, residence, his parents) · bride
(the same) · witnesses · banns or dispensation · consent of the father or guardian for minors.
*French civil example:* "L'an mil huit cent vingt, le quinze mai… ont comparu Pierre Dubois, âgé de vingt-six ans,
cultivateur, né à Gand, fils majeur de Jean Dubois et de Marie Lambert…" → "In 1820, on 15 May… appeared Pierre
Dubois, aged 26, farmer, born in Ghent, adult son of Jean Dubois and Marie Lambert…"

**Death / burial** – date of death and burial · house · name · status (widow of…) · religion · sex · age (often
rounded) · cause of death · who reported it · place of burial.
*Dutch civil example:* "…is overleden Jan de Smet, oud zeventig jaren, landbouwer, weduwnaar van Anna Peeters,
zoon van…" → "…died Jan de Smet, aged seventy, farmer, widower of Anna Peeters, son of…"

## Access periods
A register goes to the archive (and online) only when the closing period has passed – usually counted from the
**last** entry of the volume for archives, but per entry at the registry office. Typical values are 100 years for
births, 75–80 for marriages and 30–80 for deaths, but they differ by country (overview in
`countries/README.md`, details in the country guide). Younger entries are at the civil registry office; close
relatives can usually get them. Ask for a **full (literal) extract** – unlike a short certificate it includes the
margin notes. Write such requests into `data/archive_requests.md`.
Archive catalogues often list volumes still kept at the office (0 images, a note "still at the registry office") –
read that note before looking for images. Tell the user in advance that births after roughly 1910–1925 are often
not online yet.

## How a search works in practice
1. **Place → parish / civil municipality.** Small villages belonged to a parish in a neighbouring place; the
   volume description lists all villages of the parish. Search for the village in the description, not just in the
   title – and search by the village, not only by the parish from a later record: the village may have had its own
   registers (chapel of ease, filial church). A village split between two parishes has two series of books.
2. **Volume by year and type.** Watch for overlaps: one book for several villages, separate books for some villages.
   **Duplicate registers** (copies for the deanery, bishop or state, e.g. *Duplikate*, *Zweitschriften*, *doubles*)
   often hold B, M and D of one year together – useful when the original is missing, illegible or huge.
3. **Index first, then the book.** An index by letters and years gives page/folio; estimate the image (image ≈
   folio × 1 to 2) and adjust. Older indexes are sometimes off by 1–4 pages – if the entry is not on the stated
   page, look around. The offset "image – page" changes within a book (inserted leaves, missing pages).
   **Never guess the offset:** read "pag./fol." on 2–3 images, compute it, then jump (it can be +1, +2, or −19).
   For books **refoliated later**, the index refers to the original handwritten leaf number (top right), not the
   archive's foliation. If the page numbers "do not fit", look for another (ink) pagination or a second series for
   the same years (e.g. a Latin and a German series in one book).
   Old parishes often have a **summary index** over several decades – it leads quickly into the 17th century.
   Indexes follow the spelling of their time and language: names may be filed under a different initial (Czech
   O/V under W – Otava = Wotawa; Dutch IJ under Y; French De/Le prefixes under D/L or under the main word).
   Large parishes have indexes as **separate volumes** – look for them first. **When the register is missing**, the
   separately kept index may have survived: an index entry documents date, place and father – record it as `prob`
   (copy, not original) and try the register later.
4. **No index: page chronologically** – but first check that the book is not arranged by village (below). Estimate
   the position from the years on the first and last images (bisect). Old books bound from **annual extracts** may
   not be in order and years may be missing; the year is often only in the heading. Before writing "not there",
   check the year headings. Notes like "*retour à la page…*", "*vide pag.*", "*Fortsetzung*" mean the year continues
   elsewhere in the book.
   **Uncertain date** (a digit from a later note): first go through all possible years in the home parish, only then
   neighbouring parishes. (A real mistake: "9?" read as 97, five neighbouring parishes searched; the marriage was at
   home five years earlier.)
5. **Read the whole entry**, then look around (twins, other children of the same parents).
6. **The marriage was usually held in the bride's parish** (her residence) – look there first, then in the groom's.
   Some later baptisms note the date and place of the parents' marriage – check it in the marriage register.
7. **Family missing from the Catholic register? Try Protestant, Jewish or civil registers.** Many regions had
   Protestant congregations covering dozens of villages, Jewish registers, or (20th century) civil registers for
   people without religion. In mixed marriages the children's religion followed local law (e.g. in the Habsburg
   lands from 1868 sons followed the father, daughters the mother) – look for each child in both.

## Books arranged by village (common 1780–1900)
Rural parishes often kept a book **by village**: each village has its own section with its own pagination, and the
years run only within the section. Sections are not in year order, continue at the back ("*ad pag. 70*",
"*vide reliquos pag.*"), and one village's section may be at the very end. Estimating the image by year then fails
and "not there" is often a mistake (real mistakes: only the village section was searched while the marriage was in
the market town's section; the village's section was on images 380–405).
1. **Read the volume description first** (the portal's description, "contents", "territorial scope"). Descriptions
   are often incomplete, truncated or misspell village names. Find the missing sections with a book map (point 3)
   by the page numbers and village headings (often only on the first page of the section). Villages that moved
   between parishes leave gaps – search the gap in both parishes.
2. **Parish index:** it gives the village (letter or "tom." = village book) and the **page within the section**,
   not within the whole book. Image ≈ start of section + page/2 (folio/2), adjusted by the number on the image.
   Old indexes often refer to **crossed-out old leaf numbers**, and the year and leaf may be shifted by one line
   against the name – read neighbouring lines too and verify on the image. Volunteer indexes are not complete.
3. **Book map:** from every 5th–30th image only the top strip (village name, year) in low resolution, stacked with
   the image number → the structure of the whole book on one picture:
   `python3 -I tools/book_map.py <portal> <book> <from> <to> [step]` for portals with a tool (see the country
   guide), or `python3 -I tools/book_map.py files work/x_*.png` from saved images/screenshots. Then read only the
   wanted village's section at full resolution. Note the section map in the log and in the volume's
   `orientation` field (`data/volumes.json`).
4. **Column montage:** within a section crop the column with the names (groom/bride, child, village in the margin –
   at least 35 % of the page width) from 4–8 images and put them side by side
   (`book_map.py … <from> <to> 1 --side-by-side --sharp --crop x0,y0,x1,y1`); read the full entry only for
   candidates. Faded books: rather whole pages. For a quick grid of whole images use `tools/contact_sheet.py`;
   for a systematic pass through an unindexed book with a record of every image read, `tools/sieve.py`.
5. **Traps:** marriages are usually in the bride's village section, but some parishes sorted by the groom; older
   books interleave B, M and D without contents; page numbers in a separate index may not match the book. Before
   writing "not found", map **all** sections.
6. **Separate village books:** large parishes kept separate books for some villages. Search by the village of
   residence as well, and **"not found" counts only for a book that covers the village**.

## Reading the script
- **Read indexes column by column, not a whole double page at once.** When skimming the surname column a name is
  easily missed (a real mistake: "not in the marriage index 1686–1739" – it was there three times, and the line was
  then traced three generations further). Cut the full-resolution image into vertical strips and read each one
  separately, line by line: `python3 -I tools/book_map.py <portal> <book> <image> <image> --columns 4` (or
  `files <image>`; boundaries by index columns, e.g. parent – name – year – folio: `--columns 0.3,0.55,0.8`; one page
  of a double page: `--crop 0,0,0.5,1`). **Record a negative result from an index only after such reading.**
- **Read the name, date, house and page reference on the same line.** In slanted handwritten indexes it is easy to
  take the name from the neighbouring line. Indexes sometimes give the godparent instead of the child, or a wrong
  date – verify every index line in the register before reporting it.
- Decorative capitals in 17th–18th-century indexes file names elsewhere than you expect (Czech Š written as "St"
  / "Sſt", W for V and O, C/K and G/K swapped, Dutch "Ph"/"F", French "Y"/"I"). Before writing "not under this
  letter", check the letters the clerk may have used.
- **Kurrent (German cursive, ~1700–1940s)** – the commonest problem script in Central Europe:
  - Long **ſ** looks like f; **e** looks like n; **h** has a loop below the line; **u** carries a hook (˘) above it
    to distinguish it from n; **ß** is a ligature of ſ+z/s.
  - Frequent confusions: N×R, Tr×Pe, Č×G, D×B, Sch×Ch, W×St, S×B/P/D, Z×P; final "k" written almost like "r"
    ("-ek" looks like "-er").
  - Latin words (names, places, *ex*, *filius*) are usually written in **Latin script** in the middle of a Kurrent
    entry – a change of script often marks a name.
  - When unsure, **compare with the same letter elsewhere on the page written by the same hand** – even for an
    "odd" surname. Do not complete fragments to what you are looking for ("…ssil" was *Nebosstikowi* = "to the late",
    not the wanted surname). A word split over a line end ("Urlau-" / "ber") continues on the next line.
  - Second reading of "(?)" at 4–6× zoom (`tools/enhance.py … --enlarge 4`, or
    `magick x.jpg -filter Lanczos -resize 400% x4.jpg`).
- **Secretary hand / old French and Dutch hands (16th–17th c.):** "e" like "o" or "c", long ſ, "u"/"v" and "i"/"j"
  interchangeable, abbreviations with a stroke over the word (*q̄* = *que*, *ptr* = *pater*), superscript
  endings (*Joēs* = *Joannes*). Expand abbreviations only in the translation, keep them in the transcription.
- **Faded writing** (microfilm, pencil, ballpoint): if the writing is pale or the page looks almost empty, read from
  a crop with `--contrast` (`crop.py … --contrast`, `verify.py … --contrast`; or
  `magick x.jpg -colorspace Gray -contrast-stretch 2%x35% x_c.jpg`). For badly faded or stained pages
  `tools/enhance.py` makes several variants side by side; its `--strips N` mode (blur + division by the background
  of a downscaled copy + level per strip) worked best for illegible leaves; for show-through from the reverse side
  only raise the levels. **Do not declare a page empty or an entry not found** before trying contrast. Put the
  contrast crop on the website and mention it in the transcription. Green-tinted image:
  `magick x.jpg -channel G -separate -normalize x_c.jpg`.
- **Dates:**
  - Feasts instead of dates (*Dominica*, *festum S. Joannis*): *feria* = weekday (*feria 2da* = Monday), Sundays are
    named after the introit (*Dominica Laetare* = 4th Sunday of Lent); the exact date comes from a perpetual
    (liturgical) calendar for that year (Easter moves).
  - *7bris / 7ber, 8bris, 9bris, Xbris* = September, October, November, December (not months 7–10!).
  - *hujus* / *ejusdem (mensis)* = the same month as the previous entry.
  - **French Republican calendar** (France, Belgium, Luxembourg, left bank of the Rhine, 1793–1805): "an XII",
    months *vendémiaire, brumaire, frimaire, nivôse, pluviôse, ventôse, germinal, floréal, prairial, messidor,
    thermidor, fructidor* (+ *jours complémentaires*). Convert with a concordance table; write the Gregorian date in
    the data and the original in the transcription.
  - Julian vs. Gregorian calendar: Catholic regions switched 1582–1584, many Protestant regions only in 1700
    (some later); Eastern Churches kept the Julian calendar into the 20th century.
- **Names across languages:** clerks translated first names into the language of the register. Johannes / Johann /
  Jean / Jan / Joannes = John; Wenceslaus / Wenzel = Václav; Georgius / Georg / Georges / Joris / Jiří = George;
  Jacobus / Jakob / Jacques / Jacob / Jakub = James; Franciscus / Franz / François / Frans / František; Josephus /
  Joseph / Jozef / Josef; Petrus / Peter / Pierre / Pieter / Petr; Matthias / Mathieu / Matthijs / Matěj; Aegidius /
  Gilles / Gillis; Adalbertus / Adalbert / Vojtěch; Maria / Marie / Mie; Catharina / Katharina / Catherine /
  Kateřina; Anna / Anne / Anneke; Elisabeth / Isabelle / Lisbeth / Alžběta; Joanna / Johanna / Jeanne / Jana;
  Magdalena / Madeleine / Majdalena. Do not confuse similar names (Joannes ≠ Joachim).
- **Surnames are written phonetically and change** (Dubois / Du Bois / Dubos; Smet / De Smet / Desmet; Novák /
  Nowak). Search all variants. Women's surnames: in Czech/Polish records with -ová/-owa, in German/Latin often
  without (Maria Novak = Marie Nováková); in Belgium/France women keep their maiden name in civil records.
- **Place names** have older and other-language forms (German, Latin, French, Dutch, Hungarian), sometimes with
  a prefixed letter ("Volešnice" = Olešnice). Find them before searching (historical gazetteers,
  `methodology.md` → Linking records).

## Estimating years
- Ages at death or marriage are often rounded by ±2–5 years; for old people often exaggerated by 5–15 years. Write
  "c. 1756" and search for the baptism in a wider window (from −10 to +3 years of the age at death; ±3 at
  marriage). Age alone never identifies a person. Ages in years, months and days are more precise.
- Even an exact birth date in a marriage entry may have a wrong month: if the baptism is not there, go through the
  whole year ±1 and the mother's parish (children were often born at her parents').
- Brides were usually 18–28, grooms 22–32; children every 1.5–3 years.
- "Widower" at a marriage = look for the first marriage and children from it.

## Typical traps (from real research)
- The clerk got the grandfather's name wrong (Joseph instead of Wenceslas) – several entries of the same family
  decide.
- A child registered under a different first name than the family used (Frances → Anna at home).
- An adult "John" was baptised Wenceslas: an older Wenceslas died and the younger child received his name
  (necronym), and the family called him differently. In an index look for **all children of the parents** (by
  surname and house), not just the wanted name. Two baptisms with the same name usually mean the first child died.
- An index shows the "wrong" first name in the right family or house? Open the entry – the clerk may have corrected
  the name in the register (John crossed out → Francis) and the index copied the original.
- A marriage a few months before or after a birth is not an error but a wedding "just in time" – a curiosity for
  the website.
- An illegitimate child carries the mother's surname; the father is missing or added later (legitimation in the
  margin).
- The same ancestor twice in the tree (distant relatives married) – `SAME` in the data.
- Index not found ≠ entry does not exist. Write what exactly you went through.
- A marriage entry says "born according to register IV fol. 149" – the folio leads to the baptism in one download
  (folio ≈ image − the book's offset).
- Readings by helpers (sub-agents) or by other handwriting tools are always verified by you on the image
  (four-eyes principle, SKILL.md → Helpers).
- House numbers were renumbered (after fires, new building, reforms; e.g. ~1805 and 1857–1880 in the Habsburg
  lands, "alt/neu"). A different number does not mean a different family, and the same number does not mean the
  same one – keep a table house → holder by year before identifying.

## Technical traps (macOS, zsh) – put them into helper briefs too
- The shell is zsh: `for a in "x y z"; do set -- $a` does not split words (in zsh `${=a}`); `ls F*` without a match
  is an error "no matches found"; `echo ====` is an error (`=` at the start of a word). Run more complex loops via
  `bash -c '…'`.
- ImageMagick: pass the file list as separate arguments, not one variable with spaces. `-annotate` needs a font:
  `-font /System/Library/Fonts/Supplemental/Arial.ttf` (without it, "unable to read font"; the same for `montage`
  with labels). ImageMagick 6 (MacPorts, `/opt/local/bin`, command `convert`) works with the tools; add
  `export PATH=/opt/local/bin:$PATH` before commands if it is not on the PATH.
- Checking a reading: `python3 -I tools/verify.py <image> x0,y0,x1,y1 work/verify/<key> [v]` (fractions 0–1, not
  pixels) – the entry split into two halves scaled to 1900 px (a double page is unreadable otherwise). An image to
  read should be at most ~2000 px; larger images are not displayed in full.
- Make the web crop (`crop.py`) only of the **entry you read** (with column headings if needed), not of the whole
  double page – it would be unreadably small on the website. The full page is in `sources/` and on the portal via the
  link.
- Download one image per command: the network guard rejects loops of several archive calls in one command.

## Technical traps (Windows) – put them into helper briefs too
(They replace the first two macOS points; the points about `verify.py` and crops apply here too.)
- Python: `python -I -X utf8 tools/<tool>.py` (not `python3` – often a broken Microsoft Store alias). Without
  `-X utf8` piped output is in the local code page: accented letters break and scripts crash on "≈" and "→" (the
  skill's tools switch their output to UTF-8 themselves, your own one-off scripts do not – so always use `-X utf8`).
  The same applies when redirecting into a file (in Windows PowerShell 5.1 `>` even writes UTF-16 – redirect in Git
  Bash). The variable `PYTHONUTF8` does not work with `-I`.
- ImageMagick is `magick`, never `convert` (`convert` is a Windows system tool that converts disks from FAT to NTFS).
  After installing with winget, `magick` is on the PATH only after restarting Claude Code. Font for `-annotate`:
  `-font C:/Windows/Fonts/arial.ttf`.
- The command line is limited to 32,767 characters – hundreds of files (tiles) in one `magick` command fail
  (WinError 206); assemble row by row.
- Run `.sh` scripts through Git Bash (`bash tools/…`). Never edit Markdown or JSON with `sed -i` (escapes in Git Bash
  break the file) – write data following the rule "Writing data" in SKILL.md.
- Windows does not distinguish upper/lower case in file names (`o_29.jpg` = `O_29.jpg`) – distinguish preview and
  full resolution by a prefix (`preview_`, `full_`), otherwise they overwrite each other.
- Write data files in UTF-8 **without BOM** (Windows PowerShell 5.1 with `-Encoding UTF8` adds a BOM and `people.py`
  then cannot be loaded).
